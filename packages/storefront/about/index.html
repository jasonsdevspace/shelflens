ShelfLens How it works

How ShelfLens works

ShelfLens is a made-up store with a real search engine behind it. Type a few letters and it suggests products, forgives a typo, knows that trainers are sneakers, keeps the filter counts honest, holds a promotion at the top, and can say why every result landed where it did. It is built for three people: the shopper who wants the right product in a few keystrokes, the merchandiser who wants to push a promotion and see whether it worked, and the reviewer with two minutes who wants to know whether the person who built it understands search. The whole thing runs inside your browser. There is no server behind it, and what you search and open stays on your machine.

01

What happens when you type

  1. You type. A letter at a time, as fast as you like.
  2. The box waits a beat. A tenth of a second after your last keystroke it sends one request, not one per letter, and gives it a ticket.
  3. The store answers from inside your browser. A small program the page installed on your first visit holds the whole catalog and the merchandising rules. Nothing you type leaves your machine.
  4. It matches and ranks. It tidies your words the way it tidied the catalog, so accents, hyphens and plurals do not matter. It tries exact matches first, then words one typo away, then the store's synonyms. A rule can pin a promotion to the top. Then it counts the brands, colors, sizes, prices and ratings left.
  5. You see the answer to what you typed last. The answer comes back with its ticket. If a newer request has gone out since, the old answer is dropped. The address bar updates, so you can copy the page, share it, or press back.

02

What usually goes wrong, and does not here

Each of these is a mistake real stores make. Each has a test that fails on the naive version. Each link opens the demo in the right place, and your browser's back button brings you here.

The late answer

Type "trail runner" fast and the answer for "tra" can arrive after the answer for "trail". A page that shows whatever came last shows the wrong list. Here a late answer is ignored. Type "trail runner" quickly and watch the list.

The cache that forgets who is asking

A member sees lower prices from some brands, and one product a guest cannot see at all. A cache that remembers answers by the search alone would hand a member's prices to the next guest. Here the memory includes who is asking, and a test proves the leak with the naive version. Open the product a guest cannot see, then switch "Shopping as" to Member at the top of the page.

Why is this product first?

Under every result, explain mode says which of your words matched what, and which rule pinned or boosted it. Two jackets sit first because a rule pinned them. Sort by price and the pin is set aside, and the page says so instead of looking broken. Search "jacket" with explanations on, then sort by price.

Choosing Red never zeroes Blue

Tick one brand and a naive count drops every other brand to zero, leaving one choice per filter after the first click. Here each filter counts as if its own selection were not there, so the other brands keep their numbers. Tick a brand under "jacket" and watch the counts.

Nothing is still a page

When a search finds nothing, the page shows results for the phrase without the odd word, offers a spelling when a word is two typos off, offers to clear a filter that emptied the page, and falls back to the most clicked products, then the most rated. Search "zzqx jacket" and see what the page does with nothing.

A rail that never lies

Under each product is a row of related products. Until you have looked at a second product it shows the category's most rated and says so. After that it shows what you viewed, under a heading that says why. It never shows the product itself, or one you are not allowed to see. Open a product, then open one from its rail.

What you click rises, a little

A store that reorders results as people click them cannot be paged, because page two no longer agrees with page one. Here what you open counts, but only from your next visit, by a small capped amount, and never above a product a merchandiser pinned. Open a few jackets, then reload and search again with explanations on.

The Tour button in the store's top bar walks seventeen of these, each with the test behind it.

03

Under the hood, briefly

The rest is for engineers.

The engine is a TypeScript package with no dependencies, published on npm as @jasonsdevspace/shelflens. It indexes a hundred thousand products in 84 MB and answers the widest queries in about a tenth of a second. A GraphQL API sits over it and runs in a service worker in the demo, or in Node when asked, behind a typed client written in the repo so the stale response and the cache key are visible code rather than library settings. Every hard part above has a test that fails on the naive version, and every choice with a real alternative is a decision record.

The README is the case study, and the decision records are beside it.
